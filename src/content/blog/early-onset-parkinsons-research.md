I started writing this for myself. My mother was diagnosed with early-onset Parkinson's disease (EOPD) around the age of 40, and my maternal grandfather was diagnosed at 85. Sitting on the same side of the family, two generations apart, that's the kind of pattern that makes you want to read past the surface-level "Parkinson's runs in families" line and find out what the literature actually says — about my risk, about what comes next for my mother, and about whether any of the lifestyle stuff people repeat at you actually has evidence behind it.

What follows is a compressed version of that reading. I've tried to cite only peer-reviewed studies, meta-analyses, and society-level sources, and I've tried to be explicit about where the evidence is strong, where it's mixed, and where people repeat claims that don't really hold up. **This is informational synthesis, not medical advice.** If any of this is relevant to you, talk to a movement-disorders neurologist and a genetic counselor.

## 1. Genetic risk when a parent has EOPD

The general first-degree-relative baseline is well established. Across community-based studies, having any first-degree relative with PD raises your risk roughly **2–3× over the general population**. The landmark Mayo Clinic study found cumulative incidence by age 75 was **2% in first-degree relatives vs. 1% in controls** ([Marder et al., Neurology 1996](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.47.1.155)). A large 2018 Swedish population study put the adjusted relative risk at **~1.69× higher** than the general population ([PMC 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5985793/)). Familial recurrence is observed in **5–15% of PD cases** overall.

But this baseline is the wrong reference point when the affected parent has *early-onset* disease. Two features shift the prior:

- **Onset at age 40 sits squarely in the early-onset (<50) bracket**, which is heavily enriched for monogenic (single-gene) causes. A much larger fraction of EOPD cases trace to an identifiable gene than typical late-onset PD.
- **A second affected maternal-line relative** raises suspicion further, though the grandfather's onset at 85 is actually more consistent with sporadic late-onset PD than with the same gene driving an early case. He could be coincidental sporadic PD, or he could carry the same low-penetrance variant that hit my mother harder.

### The genes that actually matter for EOPD

The lifetime risk *to me* depends almost entirely on **which gene (if any) my mother carries**. The relevant ones are listed below with their inheritance pattern and what the offspring risk looks like ([Updated MDSGene review on LRRK2, npj Parkinson's 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41531-025-00881-9); [GeneReviews — PRKN](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sites/books/NBK1478/); [GeneReviews — PINK1](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK26472/); [PRKN penetrance — CHRIS cohort, Frontiers 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8382284/)):

| Gene | Inheritance | Typical onset | Lifetime risk if you inherit a pathogenic variant |
|------|-------------|---------------|---------------------------------------------------|
| **LRRK2** (esp. G2019S) | Autosomal dominant | Median ~56, ~1/3 onset <50 | Age-dependent: ~2% at age 50 → ~25–33% at age 80. Estimates span 17–80% depending on cohort and ancestry. 50% chance of inheriting from an affected parent. |
| **GBA1** | Risk variant | Variable | ~10% by 60, ~16% by 70, ~19% by 80 in PD-ascertained kin; ~2% by 65, ~11% by 85 in obligate carriers. 50% transmission per pregnancy. |
| **SNCA** | Autosomal dominant | Often early, aggressive | High penetrance, often >85%. Rare. 50% transmission. |
| **PRKN (parkin)** | Autosomal **recessive** | Median ~32 | ~100% penetrance for biallelic carriers; only ~1–25% for heterozygotes. Most common monogenic cause of EOPD (4.6–10.5% of EOPD). |
| **PINK1** | Autosomal recessive | Median ~31 | Near-complete penetrance for biallelic carriers; heterozygous risk controversial/small. |
| **PARK7 (DJ-1)** | Autosomal recessive | Median ~27 | Very rare; biallelic carriers affected. |

The LRRK2 G2019S penetrance numbers in particular are striking because they're age-dependent and modified by polygenic background and lifestyle factors. From [Brain 2024](https://academic.oup.com/brain/article/147/6/1996/7676517) and [PMC 2022 on polygenic risk modifiers](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8975556/): age-dependent penetrance for G2019S ranges from ~2% at 50 to ~33% at 80. Caffeine and other modifiers move the number meaningfully.

### Maternal-line patterns and mitochondrial DNA

Two female-line generations made me want to know if mitochondrial DNA was in play, since mtDNA is maternally inherited. The evidence here is genuinely mixed. A [2024 meta-analysis of ~13,640 PD patients vs. ~22,588 controls](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1353802024010563) did find associations with **mtDNA haplogroups B, F, H, and R** and a handful of mtDNA variants. But other large studies found **no significant difference in maternal vs. paternal transmission frequency**, and the role of mtDNA remains "unresolved" ([BMC Med Genet](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2858137/)).

Worth noting: **PINK1 and PRKN both encode mitochondrial-quality-control proteins**, so a "mitochondrial-looking" pedigree could still reflect a nuclear recessive gene rather than mtDNA itself ([Brain 2023 on mtDNA heteroplasmy in PINK1/PRKN PD](https://academic.oup.com/brain/article/146/7/2753/6881567)).

### Practical translation

- If my mother has **PRKN or PINK1 biallelic variants**: I'm an obligate heterozygote (or biallelic, if my father also happens to carry a variant — usually low population probability). Heterozygous risk: only ~1–25% lifetime, meaningfully elevated but most carriers never develop PD.
- If she has a **dominant LRRK2 variant**: 50% chance I inherited it. If I did, age-80 risk is ~25–33%, modifiable by background and lifestyle.
- If she has a **GBA1 variant**: 50% inheritance probability, ~10–20% lifetime PD risk if I inherited it.
- If **no known mutation is found** (genuinely possible — a fraction of EOPD remains genetically unexplained): my risk reverts toward the general first-degree relative baseline of 2–3× population (~2–4% lifetime).

**The single most useful next step is genetic testing for my mother.** [PD GENEration](https://www.parkinson.org/advancing-research/our-research/pdgeneration/genetics-behind-pd) (free in the US, run by the Parkinson's Foundation) covers LRRK2, GBA1, PRKN, PINK1, SNCA, PARK7, VPS35 and provides genetic counseling. Direct-to-consumer tests like 23andMe only screen for the most common LRRK2 and GBA1 variants and miss most pathogenic variants — they're not a substitute.

## 2. Stages of the disease

The standard framework is the **Hoehn & Yahr scale**, first described in 1967 and still in clinical use ([Parkinson's Foundation — Stages](https://www.parkinson.org/understanding-parkinsons/what-is-parkinsons/stages)):

| Stage | Clinical picture | Function |
|-------|------------------|----------|
| **1** | Unilateral symptoms: tremor, mild rigidity, subtle posture/facial changes on one side. | Independent, daily life largely unaffected. |
| **2** | Bilateral symptoms; trunk/neck involvement; walking difficulties and posture changes appear. | Independent, but tasks take longer. |
| **3** | Loss of postural reflexes — unsteadiness on turning, falls increase. **Pivotal disability milestone.** | Mild-to-moderate disability; still independent. |
| **4** | Symptoms severely disabling; can still stand/walk unassisted (often with aids). | Cannot live alone; significant ADL help required. |
| **5** | Wheelchair-bound or bedridden; severe rigidity. | 24-hour care required. |

Median transition times in a general PD cohort, from [Zhao et al., Movement Disorders 2010](https://movementdisorders.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/mds.22875):

- Stage 1 → 2: ~20 months
- Stage 2 → 2.5: ~62 months
- Stage 2.5 → 3: ~25 months
- Stage 3 → 4: ~24 months
- Stage 4 → 5: ~26 months

### What's different in early-onset disease

EOPD follows the same staging but progresses differently on average, and the differences are clinically important. From [Johns Hopkins](https://www.hopkinsmedicine.org/health/conditions-and-diseases/parkinsons-disease/youngonset-parkinsons-disease), [an EOPD management review (PMC 2018)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6065598/), and [Curr Neurol Neurosci Rep 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11910-025-01470-2):

- **Slower overall progression**: strong negative correlation between age of onset and time to stage 5 (r = -0.741, p < 0.001).
- **Cognition preserved much longer.** Only ~19% of YOPD patients show cognitive impairment at 18 years of disease duration, vs. the late-onset figure of ~80% with dementia within 20 years.
- **More dystonia early** — about a third of YOPD patients have off-period dystonia (foot/limb twisting) as an early sign; uncommon in late-onset PD.
- **Motor complications come faster and harder.** Motor fluctuations in **69% of YOPD vs. 46% of LOPD**, and **dyskinesias in ~91% of YOPD patients after 5 years of levodopa** — vs. much later onset in older patients. This is why doctors often delay or sequence drugs differently in younger patients.
- **Higher rates of depression and anxiety** through the course.
- **Often delayed diagnosis** because clinicians don't expect PD in a 40-year-old.

## 3. Treatment options in advanced stages

"Advanced" PD generally means: response to oral levodopa has become unpredictable (motor fluctuations), troublesome dyskinesias have appeared, or non-motor symptoms (cognition, autonomic, psychiatric) are driving disability. The main device-aided ("advanced") therapies:

### Deep Brain Stimulation (DBS)

- **Target**: Subthalamic nucleus (STN) most commonly; globus pallidus interna (GPi) is the main alternative.
- The **EARLYSTIM** trial (NEJM 2013, 11-year follow-up published 2024) randomized PD patients <61 with early motor complications to STN-DBS + medical therapy vs. medical therapy alone. The DBS group had better quality of life, motor function, and ADLs at 2 years, with persistent advantages on motor complications at 11 years ([Neuromodulation 2023](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1094715922013460)).
- **YOPD patients are excellent candidates** because slower progression and fewer non-levodopa-responsive symptoms (gait freezing, cognitive impairment) mean more years of benefit. STN-DBS in YOPD has been shown to **reduce on-medication dyskinesias by 94% at 12 months** ([DBS in EOPD, PMC 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9713840/)).
- Adaptive/closed-loop DBS — long-term feasibility reported in [JAMA Neurology 2025](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamaneurology/fullarticle/2839117) — is the most credible near-term hardware advance.

### Levodopa-Carbidopa Intestinal Gel (LCIG / Duopa)

- Continuous infusion via a PEG-J tube directly into the jejunum.
- **DUOGLOBE** 3-year and **COSMOS** long-term data ([J Neurol 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00415-023-11615-3); [PMC 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10473130/)) show sustained reductions in "off-time" from ~4.8 hrs/day to ~1.4 hrs/day, with improvement in motor fluctuations in **96%** and dyskinesia in **95%** of cases in pooled real-world data.
- A newer formulation, **LECIG** (levodopa-entacapone-carbidopa intestinal gel), broader rollout since 2024, allows lower total daily levodopa and smaller infusion volumes, with sustained >3 hr/day off-time reductions.

### Subcutaneous Apomorphine / Foslevodopa

- Apomorphine: dopamine agonist delivered subcutaneously; works within minutes for "rescue" of off-periods, or continuously via pump.
- **Vyalev (foslevodopa-foscarbidopa)** — FDA approved 2024 — continuous subcutaneous levodopa prodrug infusion. Notable addition because it avoids the GI surgery of LCIG.

### MRI-Guided Focused Ultrasound (MRgFUS)

- **Incisionless** thermoablation of the subthalamic nucleus or thalamus.
- The [NEJM 2023 randomized subthalamotomy trial](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMoa2016311) showed significant motor improvement vs. sham in tremor-dominant PD.
- A 2025 prospective cohort of 52 tremor-dominant PD patients reported ~23% relapse within one month, more often in younger patients or those with smaller lesions — important caveat ([J Clin Med 2025 review](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0929664625006588)).
- Currently best for predominantly unilateral or tremor-dominant disease; not yet a full DBS substitute.

### Multidisciplinary supportive care

Throughout advanced stages: physical therapy (LSVT-BIG), speech therapy (LSVT-LOUD for hypophonia and swallowing), occupational therapy, palliative care input. The evidence here is solid and these change function meaningfully even when drugs and surgery are doing their best.

### Emerging (not yet standard, 2025–2026)

- **Alpha-synuclein active immunotherapy** — AC Immune's **ACI-7104.056** (VacSYn Phase 2): antibody titers >20× placebo and **no UPDRS-III OFF progression at 74 weeks** in the treated arm ([PMC 2024 review of immunotherapy trials](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11638298/)).
- **Gene therapy** — multiple AAV-based approaches (AAV-GAD into STN; AAV-GDNF; AAV-neurturin) showing safety/feasibility, mixed efficacy signals ([CNS Drugs 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40263-025-01203-6)).
- **GLP-1 receptor agonists** (exenatide, lixisenatide) — being studied for disease modification; signals inconclusive but ongoing.

## 4. Side effects and complications in advanced stages

These break cleanly into motor complications (mostly drug-induced), non-motor complications (mostly disease-driven), and therapy-specific risks.

### Motor complications from long-term levodopa

- **Motor fluctuations and dyskinesia**: present in **~50% of patients after 5 years** of treatment and **~80% after 10 years**. In YOPD specifically, **91% have dyskinesia after 5 years**. These are why advanced therapies exist.
- **Wearing-off, delayed-on, no-on, and unpredictable "off" episodes** become daily challenges.
- **Off-period dystonia** — painful sustained muscle contractions, particularly mornings and feet.

### Non-motor complications

These are often the dominant burden in advanced disease ([Treatment of Advanced PD, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3038575/); [Neuropsychiatric disorders in PD, PMC 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12314238/)):

- **Cognitive decline / dementia**: ~80% within 20 years of diagnosis overall, but YOPD is notably protected (~19% at 18 years).
- **Psychosis and hallucinations**: visual hallucinations are most common. Medication-induced hallucination rates: **0–4.4% on levodopa, 7–9.3% on pramipexole** ([impulse control / hallucinations review, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3613210/)). Pimavanserin (Nuplazid) is FDA-approved for PD psychosis; clozapine and quetiapine are off-label alternatives.
- **Impulse control disorders (ICDs)** — pathological gambling, hypersexuality, compulsive shopping, binge eating — affect roughly **1 in 7 PD patients on dopamine agonists**. Usually resolve when the agonist is reduced/stopped. Particularly relevant in YOPD because dopamine agonists are often used first to delay levodopa exposure.
- **Mood**: depression and anxiety prevalence is higher than in late-onset PD; suicidality requires monitoring.

### Autonomic dysfunction

- Orthostatic hypotension (often poorly responsive to treatment)
- Constipation (often precedes motor symptoms by years)
- Urinary urgency / retention
- Erectile dysfunction
- Excessive sweating, drooling (sialorrhea)

Many of these worsen with dopaminergic drugs rather than improving.

### Sleep

REM sleep behavior disorder (acting out dreams — often predates PD diagnosis by years), excessive daytime sleepiness, restless legs, insomnia, sleep fragmentation.

### Dysphagia, aspiration, and mortality

This is the highest-stakes complication. From [Sci Rep 2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-021-86011-w) and [Eur J Neurol 2024 meta-analysis](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/ene.16449):

- PD patients have **>3× the risk of aspiration pneumonia** vs. matched controls.
- Aspiration pneumonia is the **leading cause of death in PD, accounting for ~25% of deaths**.
- After a first aspiration pneumonia event: **23.9% mortality at 1 month, 65.2% at 1 year, 91.8% at 5 years**.
- Dysphagia screening, modified diets, and speech-therapy-led swallow exercises (LSVT-LOUD, EMST) are the main mitigations.

### Therapy-specific risks

- **DBS**: ~1–3% intracranial hemorrhage; ~5% hardware infection; possible mood changes (apathy, hypomania), speech changes (especially STN), weight gain.
- **LCIG**: PEG-J tube complications — dislodgement, infection, peristomal issues; rare severe peripheral neuropathy from levodopa-induced B12/B6 depletion.
- **Apomorphine pump**: injection-site nodules, nausea (premedicated with trimethobenzamide/domperidone), hypotension.
- **MRgFUS**: transient ataxia, paresthesias, gait instability; ~23% one-month relapse in tremor PD per the 2025 cohort.

## 5. Evidence-backed risk reduction for first-degree relatives

This is the section I cared most about while writing this. Ranked by strength of evidence.

### Tier 1 — Strong, consistent evidence

#### Exercise

The single most robust modifiable factor. From [JAMA Network Open 2018 meta-analysis](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2703134) (8 studies, >500,000 participants): higher moderate-to-vigorous physical activity at ages 35–39 and in the prior 10 years was associated with significantly lower PD incidence, with a dose-response relationship. A pooled analysis of two prospective cohorts (48,574 men + 77,254 women) found **men who did ≥10 months of strenuous exercise/year had a 60% reduced PD risk**. A 2024 [npj Digital Medicine](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41746-024-01135-3) study found both consistent active (HR 0.44) and "weekend warrior" active patterns (HR 0.58) lower risk vs. inactivity. The signal is stronger in men than women in epidemiologic data.

**Practical translation**: sustained aerobic + resistance training, multiple days per week, started in midlife or earlier.

#### Avoid pesticide exposure (paraquat, rotenone, organochlorines)

A meta-analysis showed summary risk ratios of **1.62 for any pesticide exposure** and **1.52 for occupational exposure** specifically ([PMC 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3295350/)). Paraquat pooled OR **1.64**; rotenone OR **2.5** in case-control data ([PMC 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3114824/)). Biologically plausible: paraquat induces oxidative stress, rotenone inhibits mitochondrial complex I — both replicate PD pathology in animal models.

#### Avoid trichloroethylene (TCE) and chlorinated solvents

[2025 Neurology study in Medicare beneficiaries](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0000000000214174): top decile of ambient TCE exposure had **RR 1.10** for PD vs. lowest decile. The Camp Lejeune cohort (heavy water contamination): **~70% higher PD risk** decades later. TCE persists in soil and groundwater for decades.

**Practical translation**: check EPA maps for TCE plumes near home; VOC-certified water filtration if your supply is suspect; avoid hobbies/jobs involving degreasers and dry-cleaning solvents.

### Tier 2 — Strong observational evidence

#### Mediterranean / MIND diet

[Metcalfe-Roach et al., Movement Disorders 2021](https://movementdisorders.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/mds.28464): higher MIND-diet adherence associated with **later age of PD onset**. The Rush MAP longitudinal study: highest MIND tertile had **42% lower rate of incident parkinsonism**. 2024 Swedish prospective cohort: high Mediterranean adherence in women → **HR 0.69 for PD** ([npj Parkinson's 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41531-025-00932-1)).

**Practical translation**: high in vegetables, berries, whole grains, legumes, nuts, fish, olive oil; low in red meat, processed foods, butter, sweets.

#### Caffeine — especially for genetic-risk individuals

Population-level meta-analyses consistently show coffee/caffeine → lower PD risk. **For LRRK2 carriers specifically**: a [2020 Neurology metabolomic study](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0000000000010863) found plasma caffeine was **76% lower in LRRK2-mutation PD cases vs. unaffected LRRK2 carriers**, vs. only 31% lower in non-carriers. In a [4,488-subject Asian study](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lanwpc/article/PIIS2666-6065(23)00195-5/fulltext) with LRRK2 risk variants, non-caffeine-drinking carriers had **4–8× higher PD risk** than caffeine-drinking wildtype controls.

Caveat: this is observational. Trials of caffeine as therapy in established PD have been negative — the signal appears to be on *onset risk*, not progression.

#### Flavonoid / anthocyanin-rich foods (berries, tea, apples)

[Habitual flavonoid intake and PD risk, Neurology 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22491871/): men in the highest quintile of total flavonoid intake had **40% lower PD risk**. A [2022 Neurology study](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0000000000013275) (599 NHS women + 652 HPFS men): in patients already with PD, top-quartile anthocyanin intake → **66% higher survival** vs. lowest quartile.

### Tier 3 — Moderate or mixed evidence

#### Reduce dairy intake (particularly milk, particularly in men)

[2014 dose-response meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24894826/) (7 prospective studies, 304,193 subjects): highest vs. lowest dairy → OR **1.40** overall, **1.66 in men**, non-significant in women. Each 200 g/day of milk → **17% higher risk**. A larger 2026 meta-analysis (634,327 participants, 4,285 PD cases): high dairy → RR **1.21**; milk specifically → RR **1.13**. Yogurt and butter were not associated with risk. Absolute risk remains low (~1% of even the highest-consumption group developed PD).

#### Avoid head injury / TBI

[2023 meta-analysis](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13760-023-02209-x): pooled OR **1.57** for PD with prior head trauma. [2018 US veterans study](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0000000000005522): mild TBI → **56% higher PD risk**; moderate-severe → **83% higher**. Some systematic reviews dispute the causal claim for *mild* TBI; reverse causation (prodromal PD → falls → TBI) hasn't been fully ruled out.

**Practical translation**: helmet use, fall prevention later in life, avoiding repeated head impact.

#### NSAIDs (specifically ibuprofen)

A [prospective study found regular ibuprofen users had RR 0.62](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3059148/). Meta-analyses show **15% reduction** with non-aspirin NSAIDs; pooled RR for ibuprofen specifically 0.73–0.76. **No effect for aspirin or acetaminophen** — suggests something specific to ibuprofen.

**Critical caveat**: chronic NSAID use carries real harms (GI bleeding, kidney injury, cardiovascular risk). The evidence does **not** support taking ibuprofen prophylactically for PD prevention — but it's reassuring if you already need NSAIDs for another reason.

#### Vitamin D

Mixed. Mini-Finland Health Survey: low vitamin D → higher PD incidence. A meta-analysis found insufficiency → OR **1.77**, deficiency → ~2.5× risk. But the ARIC prospective cohort (17-year follow-up) found no association. Maintaining sufficiency is a reasonable general-health goal; the PD-specific case is suggestive but not settled.

### Tier 4 — Inverse association exists but NOT a recommendation

#### Smoking

One of the strongest and most reproducible inverse associations in neuroepidemiology — current and former smokers have lower PD risk ([Neurology 2020](https://www.neurology.org/doi/10.1212/WNL.0000000000009437); [Movement Disorders 2024 review](https://movementdisorders.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/mds.29707)). Mechanism unclear (nicotine? MAO-B inhibition? reverse causation?). The NIC-PD trial of nicotine in established PD was negative. **Do not start smoking.** The harms (cancer, cardiovascular disease, COPD, all-cause mortality) overwhelm any theoretical PD benefit. I'm including this only because you'll see it in the literature.

#### Higher serum urate

[Meta-analysis](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41531-022-00433-5) (449,816 participants, 14,687 cases): higher serum urate → lower PD risk, particularly in men. **Critical caveat**: the SURE-PD3 randomized trial of inosine to raise urate in PD failed — no clinical benefit. The urate-PD relationship may be a biomarker, not a target. Do not pursue urate elevation as a strategy.

### Tier 5 — Emerging

#### Air pollution avoidance

42-study meta-analysis: each 5 μg/m³ increase in long-term PM2.5 → OR **1.10** for PD ([Toxics 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2305-6304/13/2/139)). A 2024 [npj Parkinson's study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41531-024-00633-1) found the association was **stronger in those with high polygenic PD risk** — directly relevant to high-genetic-risk individuals like me. HEPA filtration indoors, avoid high-PM2.5 zones, monitor air quality.

## Prodromal surveillance — for first-degree relatives specifically

This isn't risk reduction — it's earliest possible detection, which matters because future disease-modifying therapies (alpha-synuclein immunotherapy, GBA-targeted small molecules) will likely be most effective if started before substantial neuron loss.

The four most-validated prodromal markers (MDS Research Criteria 2015/2019; [PMC 2024 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11494648/)):

1. **REM Sleep Behavior Disorder (RBD)** — acting out vivid dreams. **80–90% of polysomnography-confirmed RBD patients convert to PD, DLB, or MSA** over long follow-up. Highest specificity of any prodromal feature.
2. **Hyposmia** — reduced sense of smell. Present in **up to 90% of PD patients at motor diagnosis**; precedes motor symptoms by years (Braak stage 1 pathology in the olfactory bulb).
3. **Chronic constipation** — present in PD and prodromal PD; predates motor symptoms by up to 20 years in some cohorts.
4. **Depression / anxiety** — modest predictive value alone, additive with the above.

The Honolulu-Asia aging study showed **>2 prodromal symptoms → 4× higher PD incidence** than a single symptom alone.

If I ever develop dream-enactment behavior or progressive loss of smell, that's a reason to see a movement-disorders neurologist promptly — not to panic, but to enroll in monitoring/biomarker studies like **PPMI (Parkinson's Progression Markers Initiative)** which specifically recruits high-risk individuals.

## What I'm actually going to do

The evidence converges on a short list. If you're reading this with a similar family history, your list will look similar:

1. **Build sustained aerobic + resistance exercise** into life. Highest-evidence modifiable factor.
2. **Eat a Mediterranean / MIND-pattern diet** with explicit emphasis on berries, leafy greens, nuts, olive oil, fish.
3. **Drink coffee/tea regularly** — particularly important if my mother's genetic testing turns up LRRK2.
4. **Cut back on milk** (less critical, but a consistent observational signal).
5. **Avoid pesticide, paraquat, and TCE/solvent exposure** — occupational and residential.
6. **Don't take chronic NSAIDs for prevention**, but know the observational signal exists.
7. **Prevent head injury** — helmets, fall prevention later in life.
8. **Track prodromal symptoms** — RBD, smell, bowels — and see a neurologist if they emerge.
9. **Get my mother genetically tested** through PD GENEration or a movement-disorders clinic, with a genetic counselor.
10. **Consider enrolling in longitudinal studies** (PPMI, Fox Insight, PD GENEration). High-risk first-degree relatives are exactly who biomarker and prevention trials want.

That's roughly the shape of my own plan now. The piece I most wanted to come away with was the realisation that "genetic risk" isn't a single number — it ranges from ~3% lifetime (no identified gene; recessive gene I didn't inherit two copies of) to ~30%+ (high-penetrance dominant variant), and that spread is too wide to act on without testing. Everything else — exercise, diet, caffeine, avoiding pesticides — applies regardless of where in that range I land.
